Four spellings of your own name, across eight pages

The line that shows in a browser tab and at the top of a Google result carries your name on every page. We read all eight. Five of them say "Devine's Flowers & Botanicals". The shop page says "DeVine's Flowers & Botanicals". The shop and team page says "DeVine's Flowers and Botanicals". And the sympathy page says "Devine's Flower And Botanicals", singular, which is not your name at all. The capital V that makes DeVine yours survives on two pages out of eight.

While we were counting: your shop page, the one that takes money, carries no description tag, so Google writes its own summary of it out of whatever text it finds first.

Share your wedding page and the picture disappears

Every page carries a photo that travels with it when the link is texted, posted or pasted into a group chat. On your weddings page that photo is IMG_7751-150x150.jpg, and on your shop and team page it is another 150 by 150 file. Facebook's published minimum is 200 pixels on the shorter side; under that it drops the image and posts the bare link. So a bride who shares your work with her mother sends a gray box with a headline on it. You are a florist. The photograph is the pitch, and right now it is the one thing that does not make the trip.

You moved buildings and seven listings never heard about it

Your own site has the current address, 800 Industrial Rd. Everywhere else still says 810 Industrial Rd, the building next door: The Knot, WeddingWire, Yelp, Yahoo, the Birdeye review page, the gift-card listing, and the Choose Marshall chamber directory. We opened five of those ourselves; the other two print it in their own listing titles. Somebody who finds you through any of them and follows the map arrives at the wrong door, and the day that hurts most is the day of a funeral or a wedding.

Google reads the disagreement too. Whether a business gives the same name, address and phone everywhere is one of the things it weighs when it decides which florist to put first, and right now yours does not.

Visible on every page

None of the findings above is the real one. The real one is the distance between what happens inside 800 Industrial Road and what a stranger sees when they type your name. Inside there is a working design studio with four named people in it, locally grown and seasonally sourced stems, fifty-seven things for sale, weddings booked six months out, funerals handled with care on the worst day of somebody's life, and a plant program that keeps other businesses in town green. Which is the whole explanation for section one

Nobody spelled your name four different ways because they were careless. It happens because there is no single place in that system where your name is stored. Eight pages, each one typed by hand, by somebody who has never stood in your cooler and never will. The same reason your sympathy heading is a quotation, your events page has no events, and your delivery page has no price on it. None of that is a mistake anybody made. It is what a filled-in form looks like a year later.

The other thing worth saying plainly: your website is currently working as advertising for the company that built it. It is on their site, under their name, being shown to the next florist who calls them.

What we would do instead

You own it. All of it.

When the build fee is paid, the site is yours in writing: design, source code, content, accounts. The domain is registered in your name and transferred to you free whenever you ask. Month to month after launch, thirty days' notice, no penalty. If you ever leave, you take everything with you, working.

Three questions worth asking any web vendor, including us

1. If we stop paying you, what do we keep? 2. Whose name is every domain we have ever used registered in, the old one included, and where does each of them point today? 3. Can we see the price of a new feature before it is added? Our answers are: everything, yours, and yes. Ask us the same three in person and hold us to them.

The second question is not rhetorical, and section one is why we put it there. A web address with your name on it is an asset, the same as the sign on the building. It is worth knowing where all of yours point, and who can change that.

Section four

The part no florist software gave you.

You told us the software this industry gets feels archaic, that it promises more than it delivers, and that the things you actually wanted eyes on, your stems, your shrink, your quotes, were the things it never showed you. Weddings priced in spreadsheets. Funerals quoted across the counter with no tool at all. So before this meeting, we built the first cut of the thing you described.

Behind the counter

The workroom. Three screens, one PIN, on the same site.

An order board. Every web order lands on it by itself. Phone orders get written up in thirty seconds. Sorted by the day the flowers are needed, so a September wedding booked in June never scrolls away, and one button walks each order from confirmed to made to out the door.

Stems and shrink. Log what came in and what it cost. Log what got tossed and why. The Monday number falls out on its own: bought, tossed, your shrink percentage, and the margin on what sold that week. The visibility you said you have never had.

Quotes. Weddings built up from flowers and stem counts, the arithmetic you keep in spreadsheets today, priced live as you type. Funerals get their own tool, built for the conversation instead of a document: tap pieces at prices, watch the total against what the family said they can spend, and it prints a clean page for them and puts the order on the board before they leave. It even tells you the delivery deadline from the service time.

We will walk you through all of it in person, on your counter, and that walkthrough is the point: the wedding math in it is a stand-in until we shape it around your real spreadsheet, and the funeral price points are common industry figures waiting to be replaced with yours. The screens say so themselves rather than pretending. It gets rebuilt around how you actually work, which is a thing a form-filled template has never once done for you.

This is not a plugin and not a subscription to somebody else's system. It runs on the same site, under the same roof, owned the same way, and it is included in the monthly figure in section six. If it earns a place in your day, it grows. If a screen does not fit how you work, it gets changed, and telling us "that is not how we do it" is the most useful sentence you can say in the meeting.

Section five

The part that bills every month.

Greening is the best business on your website and it is the only one you cannot buy from it.

On-site evaluation, design and installation, then weekly maintenance for commercial clients. Your own page describes all of it and then offers a phone number.

Why this one is worth its own section. Everything else on your site is a transaction. Greening is a subscription: an office, a bank lobby, a dental practice or a restaurant that pays you every month, forever, to keep its plants alive. One of those is worth more over three years than a lot of weddings, and of everything you sell it is the only thing on your website with no front door on it.

What we would put there. A short request form built for a business rather than a shopper: what kind of space, roughly how big, how many locations, natural light or none, and a photo or two. It arrives as a brief you can price without a site visit, from the office manager who was going to think about calling you and then did not.

And the proof that sells it. Two or three of the rooms you already keep green, photographed, with the business's name on it if they will let you use it. A dentist in Marshall looking at a lobby they recognize is most of the sale finished before you speak.

We have no idea how much of your revenue Greening is today, and nothing in this letter assumes an answer. It is the first question we would ask you, because if it is a bigger number than it looks from outside, the site should be built around it rather than beside it.

Now the comparison, using your number rather than one we invented. You told us the website side alone runs you about $230 a month today, which is $2,760 a year. Ours is $150. The first year costs more than a year of staying put, because it includes the build, and the build is the part you end up owning. Every year after that is $960 less than what you pay now. Across five years it is $11,000 against $13,800, and at the end of ours the site, the store and the workroom are yours in writing. At the end of theirs you have receipts.

  • Card payments run on the Square account your counter already rings on, so online and in-store sales land in one set of books. Phone orders get their card keyed right on the order board; online checkout joins when you say so. Square's published processing rate is 2.9% plus 30¢, and each card order paid remotely carries a 99¢ order fee, paid by the customer and shown as its own line. That fee is ours: it is what keeps the ordering platform running, and it is never charged to you. Sales rung in person never carry it.

An earlier draft of this letter said that if you paid less than $150 a month today, this was not a savings pitch. Then you told us your number, so it turns out it is one. But the savings is still not the reason to do it. The reason is a better website you own outright, a store that takes orders, a workroom no floral vendor ever offered you, and a Greening form that brings in work. The $960 a year is just what it costs to say no.
