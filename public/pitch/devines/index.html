Four spellings of your own name, across eight pages

The line that shows in a browser tab and at the top of a Google result carries your name on every page. We read all eight. Five of them say "Devine's Flowers & Botanicals". The shop page says "DeVine's Flowers & Botanicals". The shop and team page says "DeVine's Flowers and Botanicals". And the sympathy page says "Devine's Flower And Botanicals", singular, which is not your name at all. The capital V that makes DeVine yours survives on two pages out of eight.

While we were counting: your shop page, the one that takes money, carries no description tag, so Google writes its own summary of it out of whatever text it finds first.

Share your wedding page and the picture disappears

Every page carries a photo that travels with it when the link is texted, posted or pasted into a group chat. On your weddings page that photo is IMG_7751-150x150.jpg, and on your shop and team page it is another 150 by 150 file. Facebook's published minimum is 200 pixels on the shorter side; under that it drops the image and posts the bare link. So a bride who shares your work with her mother sends a gray box with a headline on it. You are a florist. The photograph is the pitch, and right now it is the one thing that does not make the trip.

You moved buildings and seven listings never heard about it

Your own site has the current address, 800 Industrial Rd. Everywhere else still says 810 Industrial Rd, the building next door: The Knot, WeddingWire, Yelp, Yahoo, the Birdeye review page, the gift-card listing, and the Choose Marshall chamber directory. We opened five of those ourselves; the other two print it in their own listing titles. Somebody who finds you through any of them and follows the map arrives at the wrong door, and the day that hurts most is the day of a funeral or a wedding.

Google reads the disagreement too. Whether a business gives the same name, address and phone everywhere is one of the things it weighs when it decides which florist to put first, and right now yours does not.

The Knot, WeddingWire, Yahoo and the Choose Marshall chamber directory carry your name and an address and no way to reach your site. Not one of them has a date. A workshop with a date on it is a thing somebody can put in their calendar and pay for. A workshop described in general is a paragraph.

The sympathy page opens with a quotation instead of a heading

The main heading on your funeral page is the Helen Keller line about what we love deeply becoming part of us. It is a beautiful sentence and it is doing the wrong job: a heading is what Google reads to decide what the page is for, and yours does not contain the words a person types at the worst hour of their week. The summary Google prints underneath is worse. It reads "Funeral Arrangements are thoughtfully designed compassion", which is not a sentence.

The delivery page answers neither question a buyer has

It lists eighteen towns and a column of zip codes, which is good. It does not say what delivery costs, and it does not say by what time you have to order to get flowers there today. Those are the only two things standing between a person on your delivery page and a purchase, and both of them currently require a phone call during business hours, which is the one thing the person shopping at 9pm cannot do.

Your contact address is a Gmail account

Every page invites people to write to devinesflowersandbotanicals@gmail.com. You own a perfectly good domain name and you are not sending mail from it. A bride comparing three florists reads that address, and of everything on the page it is the detail that says small in the way you do not want. Fixing it costs nothing and takes about twenty minutes, and we do it for clients as a matter of course.

Visible on every page

4.8 stars from seventy Google reviews, and a website that reads like a directory listing.

None of the findings above is the real one. The real one is the distance between what happens inside 800 Industrial Road and what a stranger sees when they type your name. Inside there is a working design studio with four named people in it, locally grown and seasonally sourced stems, sixty-six things for sale, weddings booked six months out, funerals handled with care on the worst day of somebody's life, and a plant program that keeps other businesses in town green. Seventy people went out of their way to say so in public, and they averaged 4.8. On the screen there is a set of keyword pages, a photo too small to share, and four different spellings of DeVine. The flowers are the best advertising you will ever own, and your website is where they go to get smaller. Closing that gap is the entire reason for this letter.

Section two

The part that matters most.

You are a portfolio item

Your footer reads "Designed by: Creative Web Designing, Inc." They are a website company in Coldwater whose portfolio is sorted into industry categories: florists, electricians, tree removal, heating and cooling, restaurants, real estate, construction. You are filed under Florist. There is one other business in there with you, Neitzert's Greenhouse.

Open both sites and you are looking at the same website twice. Same WordPress, same WooCommerce store, same grid of category tiles, same footer, same credit line, right down to the copyright reading "© [name] 2026" in the same position. Yours is a florist-shaped form that somebody filled in with your words.

Their florist portfolio, with you in it ↗ The other florist ↗

Which is the whole explanation for section one

Nobody spelled your name four different ways because they were careless. It happens because there is no single place in that system where your name is stored. Eight pages, each one typed by hand, by somebody who has never stood in your cooler and never will. The same reason your sympathy heading is a quotation, your events page has no events, and your delivery page has no price on it. None of that is a mistake anybody made. It is what a filled-in form looks like a year later.

The other thing worth saying plainly: your website is currently working as advertising for the company that built it. It is on their site, under their name, being shown to the next florist who calls them.

What we would do instead

You own it. All of it.

When the build fee is paid, the site is yours in writing: design, source code, content, accounts. The domain is registered in your name and transferred to you free whenever you ask. Month to month after launch, thirty days' notice, no penalty. If you ever leave, you take everything with you, working.

Three questions worth asking any web vendor, including us

1. If we stop paying you, what do we keep? 2. Whose name is every domain we have ever used registered in, the old one included, and where does each of them point today? 3. Can we see the price of a new feature before it is added? Our answers are: everything, yours, and yes. Ask us the same three in person and hold us to them.

The second question is not rhetorical, and section one is why we put it there. A web address with your name on it is an asset, the same as the sign on the building. It is worth knowing where all of yours point, and who can change that.

Section three

What we would build.

One site, written by hand, on your own domain, replacing everything you have now. No templates and no page builder, which is why the things in section one cannot happen on it.

What is in it

A shop that behaves like a florist, not like a catalog. All sixty-six arrangements across your eight categories, carried over with your photography, and then the two things the current store does not do: a delivery box that takes a zip code and answers yes we come to you, here is what it costs, and a date picker that knows what time it is. Order by the cutoff and it offers today. Order after it and it offers tomorrow, so nobody buys a same-day delivery at midnight and nobody has to call to find out.

Checkout that does not rent anything. Card payment runs on Stripe's own page, so card numbers never touch your website and there is nothing on it for anyone to steal. There is no store plugin, no store subscription and no per-feature upsell underneath it. What you pay is at the end of this letter and that is all of it.

Weddings, sold the way you actually sell them. Your five-step process is already good copy and it stays. What goes on top of it is a real inquiry form: date, venue, rough size, colors, a photo upload for the pictures every couple has saved on their phone anyway. It arrives in your inbox as a filled-in brief instead of an email that says "hi, do you do weddings," and it books the consultation you already ask people to schedule six months out.

Sympathy handled with care. The Keller line stays, because it belongs on that page. It stops being the heading. Underneath it goes the thing a family actually needs at 11pm: the arrangements, what each one is, delivery to the funeral home by a stated time, and a card message field that does not make them phone a stranger to say it out loud.

Events with dates on them. A workshop is a date, a price, a number of seats and a button. The same page runs the seasonal events and the market tours, and when a date passes it takes itself off the page. Nobody has to remember to do that.

Your four designers, on the page, by name. Gayle, Becky, Lacey and Shawna are the reason people come back, and a small photo and two real sentences each does more for a wedding inquiry than any paragraph about quality ever written.

The name spelled DeVine, everywhere, once. Your business facts live in exactly one file on the new site: the name, the address, the hours, the phone. Change it there and it changes on every page at the same moment. Section one cannot repeat itself, because there is nowhere for a second spelling to hide.

Mail from your own address. hello@devinesflowersandbotanicals.com, or whatever you prefer, set up with your existing inbox so nothing about your day changes. This one is free and we do it whether or not you buy anything else.

Built to a standard, and here is how we know

  • It is measured, not eyeballed. Contrast, text size and tap targets are checked against the accessibility standard at four screen widths before launch, because your customers are not all twenty-five.
  • It opens fast on a phone with one bar. That is the phone standing in a parking lot deciding whether to order sympathy flowers from you or from the florist listed above you.
  • Every page shares properly. A real photograph, sized right, on every page a customer might text to somebody. Section one, finding two, made permanent.
  • Nothing rented on top. No plugin subscriptions, no page builder license, no per-feature upsell.

We build the concept site first, before you owe anything, so that this section stops being a description and becomes something you can open on your phone. Say the word at the bottom of this letter and that is the next thing that happens.

Section four

The part that bills every month.

Greening is the best business on your website and it is the only one you cannot buy from it.

On-site evaluation, design and installation, then weekly maintenance for commercial clients. Your own page describes all of it and then offers a phone number.

Why this one is worth its own section. Everything else on your site is a transaction. Greening is a subscription: an office, a bank lobby, a dental practice or a restaurant that pays you every month, forever, to keep its plants alive. One of those is worth more over three years than a lot of weddings, and of everything you sell it is the only thing on your website with no front door on it.

What we would put there. A short request form built for a business rather than a shopper: what kind of space, roughly how big, how many locations, natural light or none, and a photo or two. It arrives as a brief you can price without a site visit, from the office manager who was going to think about calling you and then did not.

And the proof that sells it. Two or three of the rooms you already keep green, photographed, with the business's name on it if they will let you use it. A dentist in Marshall looking at a lobby they recognize is most of the sale finished before you speak.

We have no idea how much of your revenue Greening is today, and nothing in this letter assumes an answer. It is the first question we would ask you, because if it is a bigger number than it looks from outside, the site should be built around it rather than beside it.

It is a pitch for a better website that you own outright, with a store that answers the delivery question and a Greening form that brings in work. Compare it on that.
